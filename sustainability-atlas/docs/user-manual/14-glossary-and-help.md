# 14 — Glossary and help

This chapter covers the places to go when something is unclear: the glossary of terms, the help menu
and the guided tour, the feedback button, and a short pointer list telling you which chapter of this
manual answers which kind of question.

## The glossary

The Glossary page holds plain-language definitions for the carbon-market and Hedera terminology used
throughout the Atlas. Reach it from the sidebar, or from the **?** button in the top bar via
**Glossary of terms**.

### Finding a term

Type in the search box to filter the list as you type; the count above it updates to show how many
terms match. If nothing matches, the page says so and names the text you searched for, so you can see
whether you have a typo.

Five buttons filter the list by subject — **All Terms** plus four categories:

| Category | Covers |
|---|---|
| **All Terms** | Everything. |
| **Carbon Markets** | Issuance, retirement, vintage, registry, methodology, sectoral scope, crediting period, tCO2e, active supply, transfers, standards, developers and the credit lifecycle. |
| **Blockchain & Hedera** | Hedera Guardian, policies, on-chain data, topic ids, mainnet and testnet, DIDs, verifiable credentials, fungible tokens and NFTs, token supply, HashScan, IPFS and schemas. |
| **SDG** | The Sustainable Development Goals and how projects tag them. |
| **Platform & Data** | Terms specific to the Atlas itself: watchlists, custom charts, the Widget Library, raw data, mock data, guest view, sync status, quick filters, and the System User and System Administrator roles. |

Choosing a category updates the count to show how many terms it contains.

### Reading an entry

Each term shows a one-line definition. Click it to expand a fuller explanation — usually the part that
actually answers the question, since the short line is designed to fit on one row.

Underneath an expanded entry, a **Related:** row offers chips linking to neighbouring terms. Clicking
one jumps to that term and expands it. This is the most useful thing on the page: concepts like
*vintage*, *issuance* and *retirement* only really make sense against each other, and the related
chips let you follow the thread rather than searching each one separately.

### Where these definitions show up

A panel at the bottom of the page explains the other half of the glossary: small info icons sit next
to field labels throughout the Atlas — on stat cards, table headers, project key facts and filter
labels — and each one opens the same short definition in place. You rarely need to come to the
glossary page deliberately; the definitions come to you where the term is used. The page exists for
when you want to read around a subject rather than look one word up.

## The help menu and the guided tour

The **?** button in the top bar is present on every page, for everyone, signed in or not. It offers
two things: **Take the product tour** and **Glossary of terms**.

The guided tour is described in full in chapter 01. In brief: it is a one-minute walk through the
interface, it starts by itself the first time you sign in, and after that it is always a deliberate
choice. It never restarts itself. If you skipped it and want it back, this button is where it lives —
that is the entire reason it sits in the top bar rather than buried in settings.

Signed-in users have a second route: **Account Settings** → **Product tour** → **Replay the tour**.

## Sending feedback

A **Feedback** button is anchored in the bottom-right corner of every page. It opens a short form:

- The **Page** you were on is filled in automatically, so you do not have to describe where you were.
- **Description** is the only required field.
- **Email** is optional. It is used solely to tell you when the thing you reported has been dealt
  with — if you leave it blank, your report is still read, you just will not hear back.
- **Capture screen** attaches an image of the current page. It is worth using for anything visual: a
  chart that renders oddly, a layout that breaks, a number that looks wrong in context. **Remove
  screenshot** takes it off again if you change your mind.

A good report says what you did, what you expected and what happened instead. Two details make an
enormous difference and are almost always missing: **which network you were on** (mainnet or testnet)
and **the identifier of the specific record** you were looking at — a project name, a token id, a
registry name. Without those, a report about a wrong number usually cannot be reproduced.

If something looks stale rather than wrong, check the **Data synced up to** timestamp in the sidebar
first, and mention it in the report. Chapter 12 explains what it means.
